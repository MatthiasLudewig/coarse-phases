(article-big-family)=
# Big family

A single subset of a coarse space does not encode a support condition invariant
under {ref}`thickening <def-fattening>`. The appropriate replacement is the collection of all subsets
allowed by that support condition.

## Definition

Let $X$ be a {prf:ref}`bornological coarse space <def-bornological-coarse-space>`.

```{prf:definition} Big family
:nonumber:
:label: def-big-family

A *big family* on $X$ is a nonempty collection $\mathcal Y\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$
closed under subsets, finite unions, and {ref}`coarse thickening <def-fattening>`.
```

The family need not cover $X$.
In particular, $\{\varnothing\}$ is a big family.
The bornology of $X$ and the whole power set are also big families.

(sec-elementwise-intersection-union)=
## Elementwise intersection and union

For big families $\mathcal Y,\mathcal Z$, define their elementwise intersection, respectively union, by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal Y\Cap\mathcal Z
 &=\{Y\cap Z:Y\in\mathcal Y,\ Z\in\mathcal Z\},\\
\mathcal Y\Cup\mathcal Z
 &=\{Y\cup Z:Y\in\mathcal Y,\ Z\in\mathcal Z\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both are big families.

Because the families are downward closed, $\mathcal Y\Cap\mathcal Z$ is also
the ordinary set-theoretic intersection $\mathcal Y\cap\mathcal Z$.
The operation $\Cup$, however, is generally larger than ordinary union: it is the smallest big family containing both families.
These operations give a distributive lattice of support conditions.

## Example: Families generated by subsets

```{prf:definition} Generated big family
:nonumber:
:label: def-generated-big-family

For $A\subseteq X$, write

$$
\{A\}=\{Y\subseteq X:\exists E\in\mathcal C_X,\ Y\subseteq A_E\}.
$$

This is the *big family generated by $A$*: the smallest big family containing $A$.
```

Here braces denote a big family, not the singleton collection consisting of
$A$. For a metric space, its members are the subsets of finite-radius
neighbourhoods of $A$. For example, the family generated by a line in
$\mathbb R^2$ consists of subsets of strips of finite width around that line.

One has $\{A\}\Cup\{B\}=\{A\cup B\}$. In contrast,
$\{A\cap B\}\subseteq\{A\}\Cap\{B\}$ can be strict. For the two disjoint
parallel lines $\mathbb R\times\{0\}$ and $\mathbb R\times\{1\}$, the
right-hand side is the family of strips, whereas the left-hand side is
$\{\varnothing\}$.

The *coarse boundary* of a subset $A$ is the big family

$$
\partial A:=\{A\}\Cap\{X\setminus A\}.
$$

It records the controlled overlap of the two sides.
It is used in {ref}`coarse transversality <article-coarse-transversality>` and coarse excision.


## Example: Sparse subsets

```{prf:definition} Sparse subset
:nonumber:
:label: def-sparse-subset

A subset $S\subseteq X$ is *sparse* if $X\setminus S_E$ is coarsely dense for
every entourage $E$.
Here $A$ is *coarsely dense* if $A_F=X$ for some entourage $F$.
```

For a metric space this says: for each $r$ there is an $R$ such that every
point of $X$ lies within distance $R$ of $X\setminus S_{E_r}$. Thus every fixed
thickening of $S$ has bounded width. A line in $\mathbb R^2$ is sparse; the
integer lattice in $\mathbb R$ is not, since a fixed thickening fills the line.

For metric coarse structures the sparse subsets form a big family.
Closure under subsets and thickening is immediate.
Here is a direct verification of closure under unions.
Given sparse $S,T$ and $r$, choose $a$ so that every point is within $a$ of $X\setminus S_{E_r}$. Then choose $b$ so that every point is
within $b$ of $X\setminus T_{E_{r+a}}$. Starting at $x$, first choose such a
point $y$ outside $T_{E_{r+a}}$, and then choose $z$ within $a$ of $y$ outside
$S_{E_r}$. The triangle inequality shows that $z\notin T_{E_r}$. Thus the
complement of $(S\cup T)_{E_r}$ is $(a+b)$-dense. Radii may be enlarged slightly
if the infimum defining distance is not attained.
